Earth Platform build 66: a map and compass for survival, Earth advancements, ocean currents to sail by, natural sea cliffs and river banks, and far terrain that costs far less to draw while you move.

Needs Minecraft 1.21.1 and NeoForge 21.1.256 or newer. For shaders, add Iris 1.8 and Sodium 0.8 and a Voxy-capable pack such as Photon v1.3b. Give the game about 6 GB of memory with shaders.

New since build 65:
- Forested river banks no longer stand over the water in sheer grey stone walls, as all along the Amazon, where the real banks are a few metres high. Outside the United States and a few other countries the elevation data reads the top of a forest's canopy as the ground, so wherever forest meets a river or the sea the land stood 12 to 26 blocks over the water in a cliff, near and far alike; from a distance it showed as a grey band with pale stripes under the trees. Banks were already eased down to the water up to 14 blocks high; forested banks now ease from up to 24 blocks higher still, so the forest slopes down to the water instead. Grass and clearings among the trees ease with them, rather than standing up as pillars. Mountain valleys keep their shores as before. Ground already generated near you keeps its old banks; ground generated from now on, and all far terrain, gets the new ones.
- Replay Mod recordings now show you while you ride the jet stream. Before, a replay drew the gliding player about three and a half ticks behind the recorded path (some 200 blocks at jet speed), and only within about 38 blocks of the camera, so a camera following the path never saw them. Other players' gliders in multiplayer keep up the same way. In a replay a fast glider is now drawn about 1.5 ticks (77 ms) behind its recorded path.
- Under shaders, the far ocean no longer shows pale patches where the water went missing in rows of far cells. Far water's floor now sits behind the water's surface as the game's own coarse depth holds it, so packs measure the same water depth on every row.
- A gliding player no longer blinks out for single frames, which showed most in Replay Mod renders of glides. Flying straight, as the jet stream carries you, rounding could put the angle the game turns a glider by out of range for a frame, and the whole player vanished from it. Gliders in replays also wobble about half as much from frame to frame.
- A firework rocket can no longer freeze the game. A rocket that rose out of the ground the server had loaded around you, such as one left behind when a rocket climb outran it, was held still there forever and never exploded, so it was saved with the world. Your game kept flying it on, faster every tick, until working out what it might hit took so long the game stopped, and it did so again each time you came back. Rockets now burn out on time wherever they are, and in the Earth a runaway rocket can't fly faster than 16 blocks a tick.
- `/earthperf export` saves your performance log as one zip, `earth-performance-<date>.zip` under `earthplatform/perf/exports/` in the game folder, and links the folder in chat, so you can attach it to a bug report. It only saves the file; nothing is sent anywhere. What the log holds, and what it never holds, is in the new privacy page (docs/PRIVACY.md).
- A map and compass for survival. Players who can't use the travel map now get a map on **N** too: the same globe, centered on you, that never moves anyone. Click a spot, a famous place or another player and press **Point compass here**, and a compass you carry points there; one pointed at a player follows them. With a compass in hand the action bar says what it points to and how far away that is, as in "Mount Fuji, Japan · 8,555 km". `/earth compass` does the same from chat (`to`, `place`, `here`, `player`, `home`, and `spawn` to make it plain again). Server operators can switch it off with `/earth settings survivalMap off`.
- A plain compass on the Earth now points to your spawn. Before, it pointed to 0° N 0° E, in the Gulf of Guinea off West Africa, wherever you were. A recovery compass also shows how far away you died. During a Where on Earth round every compass spins and shows no distance, so it can't give the answer away.
- An Earth tab of advancements, for going places on the real Earth: ground 2,000, 4,000 and 8,000 meters up, 50 meters below sea level as in Death Valley, 100 and 1,000 meters down in the sea, the equator and the date line, both polar circles, 2, 10 and 50 countries, 5,000 km from your spawn and the far side of the Earth from it, and all the way round the world in one trip, which shows its progress every 10 degrees. The jet stream advancement moves into the tab, and two more go with compasses. Heights go by the ground itself, so towers and mine shafts don't count.
- Ocean currents. Out on the open sea, sprint while you paddle a boat forward and an ocean current carries it the way it faces at about 900 blocks a second, so you can sail to another continent in survival in an hour or two, as the jet stream lets you fly there. Like the real surface currents it runs a little faster westward in the tropics and eastward in the middle latitudes. Let go of the keys and it keeps you; steer with **A** and **D**. The boat looks ahead and slows to a stop before land or sea ice, and back slows it to a stop whenever you like. Riding one earns a new Earth advancement.
- Earth Platform has its own page in Sodium's Video Settings. With Sodium installed, the **Earth** page beside Sodium's own sets the far terrain's distance (in km and miles), sharpness and textured levels, the cloud height, the download note and whether Create World starts on Earth, and opens every other Earth setting in one click. They are the same settings as the config screen and the pause menu, so a change in one shows in the others. Distance and textured levels still apply after a restart, and Sodium says so.
- Far terrain costs much less to draw while you move or turn. Each time the view changed, the game worked out again which far columns to draw across the whole far pyramid, tens of thousands of them, which often took longer than drawing them. Now it keeps that, and works out again only the part that changes: as you move, the columns where one level of detail hands over to the next; as far terrain arrives or finishes fading in, the columns under it. In between it only puts what is on screen in order, starting from the last frame's order. Far water draws from a few shared buffers instead of one per column, and where nearby terrain takes over from far terrain is worked out from what changed instead of afresh. Far terrain that arrives in the meantime joins at most a tenth of a second later. High up, and as fast as the jet stream or an ocean current carries you, where the far terrain nearest you is drawn coarser, it waits until you have crossed 128 to 2,048 blocks instead of 64. At the Sharpest setting on our test machine, the far terrain took 7 ms of each frame instead of 43 flying over Yosemite, 3 ms instead of 46 turning on the spot, and 11 ms instead of 91 walking on the valley floor. The picture is the same.
- The farthest far terrain of a new place is made a little faster. It no longer looks for reservoirs around land that has no water mapped near it, where there can't be any, with the same terrain as before: at Yosemite the farthest level takes 13% less time and the whole far view 5% less.
- The ground around you is ready sooner. Each 16-block cube of nearby ground is turned into something the game can draw about a quarter faster: blocks buried on every side are passed over instead of asked for their faces, and light is worked out faster where a cube is all one block. Arriving on Yosemite's valley floor, the ground around the player was all ready to draw after 66 seconds on our test machine instead of 85 to 97, each cube taking 22 ms instead of 30. Far terrain is also meshed about 13% faster. The blocks and their light are the same.
- Far terrain no longer covers the ground at your feet while it loads. Joining a world or arriving somewhere new, the far terrain filling in for ground still on its way was drawn over the blocks already there, so big blocky shapes and striped patches stood around and under you for several seconds. Now, while the ground around you loads, far terrain within your render distance fills in only where the game hasn't drawn its own blocks yet. Once the ground has loaded, far terrain draws as before, at no extra cost.
- Sea cliffs no longer carry patches of water hanging on their faces, as on Morocco's Rif coast. Where the elevation data rises out of the sea a little offshore, the strip of sea along the shore was taken for a lake on top of the cliff, and its water poured down the cliff face as a box of falling water. That strip is now the sea at sea level, and steep steps in the data under the open sea no longer stand up as stone walls offshore, as off the Cliffs of Moher. Ground already generated near you keeps its old shore; ground generated from now on, and all far terrain, gets the new one.
- Sea cliffs no longer stand in one straight wall along the coast. Where the ground rose straight out of the sea, a sheer face the same height all along the shore, as on Morocco's Rif coast and the Cliffs of Moher, the face now breaks back from the water: steep and stepped in places, near sheer in others, in ledges a few blocks high, with coves, headlands and little beaches along its foot. Beaches and gentle shores keep their ground. Ground already generated near you keeps its old cliffs.
- Sea cliffs under a flat clifftop, as the cliffs of Dover, the Seven Sisters, Old Harry Rocks and the Twelve Apostles, stand as sheer faces again. The elevation data blurs such a cliff into a smooth slope forty-odd blocks wide; that slope is now cut back into a sheer face that wanders in and out in bays and buttresses, with the beach at its foot. Its top follows the land behind it, dipping where valleys reach the sea, and rounds over at the edge. Cliffs also break back from beaches and shore platforms, not only from the water. Mountainsides that really are slopes, as at Big Sur, Amalfi and Slieve League, keep their shape. Ground already generated near you keeps its old cliffs.
